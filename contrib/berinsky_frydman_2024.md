# Measuring Attentiveness in Self-Administered Surveys

**Contributor:** Jaepyo Yang

**Full citation:** Berinsky, Adam J., Alejandro Frydman, Michele F. Margolis, Michael W. Sances, and Diana Camilla Valerio. 2024. "Measuring Attentiveness in Self-Administered Surveys." _Public Opinion Quarterly_ 88(1): 214–241. doi:10.1093/poq/nfae004.

---
## 1. What the paper establishes

In self-administered surveys, how do researchers identify inattentive responses? This paper answers this question by reviewing different ways to measure attention. Direct measures include instructional manipulation checks and bogus items. Indirect measures use mechanisms such as response times and patterns. The paper also shows the disagreements about how these measures should be used. There isn’t a consensus on a preferred measure or cutoff. The authors recommend that researchers explain their choices, document how they handled inattention, and report how these decisions affect their results. Rather than proposing a new estimator, this paper focuses on reviewing existing research.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether the attention measures and screening decisions are reported.** If a manuscript measures attention or screens respondents, look for the indicators used. What’s the pass rule or cutoff? How many respondents were flagged or excluded? When was screening conducted? Compare the number of survey respondents with the number in the analysis. Check what explains the difference. If it is unexplained, investigate further. 

**(b) Check whether handling inattention changes the estimates.** Ask for the main estimates and the demographic composition of both the full and attentive-only samples. If respondents are down-weighted, compare the estimates before and after weighting. Also ask how the weights were chosen. In experiments, check for overgeneralization. An attentive-only estimate may not apply to the general public, where some people may not pay attention to the treatment. Excluding inattentive respondents could make treatment effects look larger. The estimated effect may also be smaller in the full sample because some respondents did not fully read the treatment materials.

**(c) If a survey experiment is conducted, check whether attention measures were collected before the treatment.** Conditioning on a post-treatment attention check can undermine the benefits of randomization. Pay special attention when respondents are excluded for answering questions about the treatment incorrectly. If attention was measured only after treatment, use the full-sample treatment effect as the main result. Additionally, response times or response patterns measured before treatment could be used for a robustness check. However, these measures cannot establish whether respondents actually paid attention to the treatment.

**(d) Check whether the attention measures are valid for the behaviors they are intended to detect.** Different measures capture different forms of inattention. For example, a straightlining index may identify respondents who repeatedly select the same answer but miss those who respond randomly. Response-time measures may also miss distracted respondents who take unusually long to complete a survey. Reviewers should therefore ask whether the measures are appropriate for the study and supported by validation evidence.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors ask researchers to disclose “research decisions that affect the size and makeup of the sample” (p. 215). Different attention measures detect different forms of inattention (p. 225). Question difficulty, placement, and cutoff choices affect who gets flagged (pp. 222–223, 226–227). The authors therefore recommend reporting the measures used, who was flagged, and any cutoffs applied (p. 235).

**For (b).** The authors recommend reporting results from both the full and attentive-only samples, along with their demographic composition (pp. 232, 235). Attentive respondents tend to be older, whiter, and more educated, though these differences are generally small (p. 231). In their example of campaign ads, excluding inattentive respondents “may result in inflated treatment effect estimates” because real audiences often ignore campaign messages (p. 233). They also note that inattentive respondents assigned to treatment may not have actually received it in a meaningful sense (p. 219, n. 2). The review also discusses dropping and reweighting as a possible way to handle inattention (p. 231), providing a basis for checking how weighting decisions affect the estimates.

**For (c).** The authors suggest that “the direct measure comes before the treatment is administered to avoid the risk of posttreatment bias” (p. 223). This concern is especially relevant to factual manipulation checks, which ask respondents questions about the treatment content and are therefore typically administered after treatment (p. 221). Excluding respondents based on these checks may introduce bias. In this sense, I suggest using pre-treatment attention measures when available.

**For (d).** The authors say that different response-pattern measures “do not all identify the same respondents as inattentive” because they capture different behaviors (p. 225). For example, straightlining measures identify repeated identical responses, while other methods detect inconsistent answers (p. 225). They also note that inattentive respondents may complete surveys either “too fast or too slow.” This can limit measures based only on unusually fast responses (p. 226). The paper therefore emphasizes selecting measurement strategies that have already been carefully validated (p. 228). The authors also argue that researchers who develop new measures should establish their validity and reliability (pp. 228–229).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** Studies using self-administered surveys, when attention is measured or used to screen respondents. Check (a) concerns disclosing attention measures and exclusion decisions. Check (b) applies when respondents are excluded or reweighted based on attention. Check (c) focuses on survey experiments in which attention measures are used to select respondents or compare treatment effects across groups. Check (d) is relevant when researchers use attention measures, particularly indirect or newly developed measures whose validity needs to be established.

**Do not automatically require attention checks in every online survey.** This paper also says that doing nothing and keeping all respondents could be a possible strategy (p. 231). The authors also say that there is no single solution to handling inattention (p. 216). A manuscript should not be criticized simply because it does not include an attention check. 

**Do not exclusively prescribe a particular attention measure or cutoff.** The paper has not reached a consensus on the optimal number, difficulty, or placement of attention checks (p. 223). However, researchers should still justify their choices and use validated measures (pp. 228–229). Rather than requiring one specific method, reviewers should assess whether a measure is appropriate.

**Do not treat every post-treatment attention check as a source of bias.** This concern is related to check (c). Having a post-treatment manipulation check does not automatically create selection bias. However, using its results to exclude respondents may introduce bias. Additionally, pre-treatment response times or response patterns may provide an alternative if available.

**Do not assume a fixed direction of bias.** Excluding inattentive respondents may inflate treatment effects when the study concerns real-world messages that people often ignore (p. 233). However, the authors also discuss experiments where the treatment is used to induce something else, such as emotion, and adjusting for inattention may be justified (p. 233). The consequences depend on the research question and design.

**Do not extend these checks uncritically to interviewer-administered surveys or dishonest responding.** The review focuses on self-administered surveys (p. 215) and distinguishes inattention from dishonest responses (p. 219).

## 5. What failure looks like

In a methods footnote, the manuscript says respondents who failed the attention check were removed. However, the question, failure rule, and the number excluded are missing. The reported N is smaller than the fielded N, with no explanation for the difference. Every table uses only the screened sample, without full-sample estimates or a comparison of demographic composition. If respondents are down-weighted, the paper does not explain why certain weights were used or how they affected the results. The paper also uses a response-time cutoff without showing whether it can identify inattentive respondents. In a survey experiment, respondents are excluded for answering factual questions about a vignette incorrectly after seeing it. The manuscript then overgeneralizes the attentive-only treatment effect to the public. A reviewer should ask for the missing information, but should not assume that these problems necessarily bias the estimates in a particular direction.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Press, Daryl G., Scott D. Sagan, and Benjamin A. Valentino. 2013. “Atomic Aversion: Experimental Evidence on Taboos, Traditions, and the Non-Use of Nuclear Weapons.” _American Political Science Review_ 107(1): 188–206. Respondents read a news-style experimental article and answered a question about its content. Those who failed could reread the article, and responses that failed the check were excluded. Aronow, Baron, and Pinson (2019, _Political Analysis_ 27(4), sec. 3) discuss this exclusion, note that the number excluded was unavailable, and replicate the study while retaining inattentive respondents. Checks (a), (b), and (c) apply because a post-treatment check determined inclusion, and the published estimates lacked a corresponding full-sample comparison. The replication found a stronger result when inattentive respondents were retained, showing why reviewers should not assume the direction of the difference.

**Should NOT trigger:** Lyall, Jason, Graeme Blair, and Kosuke Imai. 2013. “Explaining Support for Combatants during Wartime: A Survey Experiment in Afghanistan.” _American Political Science Review_ 107(4): 679–705. The study uses a randomized endorsement treatment (p. 688), with local enumerators administering the questionnaire in person (p. 683, n. 10) to 2,754 men in 204 villages (pp. 684–685). The paper discusses refusals and “don’t know” answers and accounts for missing responses in its analysis (p. 689). It does not describe an attention screen used to exclude respondents. These features do not warrant the attention-screening checks above. This case tests whether reviewers look for the specific sample-selection practices discussed by Berinsky et al. before applying these checks to a survey experiment.
