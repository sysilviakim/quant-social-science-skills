# Robust Nonparametric Confidence Intervals for Regression-Discontinuity Designs

**Contributor:** Jina Kim

**Full citation:** Calonico, Sebastian, Matias D. Cattaneo, and Rocío Titiunik. 2014. "Robust Nonparametric Confidence Intervals for Regression-Discontinuity Designs." *Econometrica* 82(6): 2295–2326. doi:10.3982/ECTA11757.

## 1. What the paper establishes

RD studies typically estimate the average treatment effect at the cutoff with local linear regression inside a bandwidth chosen by a data-driven selector; because such selectors target point estimation, the chosen bandwidth is "large," leaving an approximation bias that is not negligible relative to the standard error. Conventional confidence intervals, which ignore this bias, fall short of their nominal coverage and over-reject the null of no effect. The authors propose robust bias-corrected (RBC) confidence intervals, which subtract an estimate of the bias and use a new standard error that also accounts for the variability of that bias estimate; these intervals remain valid with the same bandwidths and apply to sharp, fuzzy, and kink RD designs. In the authors' simulations, conventional intervals covered the true effect as rarely as about 30% of the time in one design, while RBC intervals improved coverage by about 10–15 percentage points on average, bringing it close to the nominal level at the cost of somewhat longer intervals.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) If a local polynomial RD manuscript uses a bandwidth chosen by an MSE-optimal selector (e.g., Imbens–Kalyanaraman or CCT), by cross-validation, or by the researcher without showing that it is small enough for the bias to be negligible, check whether its confidence intervals and p-values are conventional (τ̂ ± 1.96 × SE, with no bias correction) or robust bias-corrected.** If they are conventional, ask for RBC results. With such bandwidths, conventional intervals undercover, so the error runs toward overstating significance.

**(b) If the manuscript reports a "bias-corrected" estimate or interval, check whether its standard error is the robust one that accounts for the uncertainty of the bias estimate, or the conventional standard error carried over from before the correction.** The "Bias-corrected" row of `rdrobust` output is the latter; the "Robust" row is the authors' method.

**(c) If the manuscript says it uses "robust standard errors" for its RD estimates, check whether this means robust bias-corrected inference or heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors from a regression run inside the bandwidth.** The latter neither corrects the bias nor accounts for the variability of a bias estimate, so it is conventional inference and check (a) applies.

**(d) If the manuscript reports conventional intervals as its main results because the RBC intervals are "too wide," or relegates RBC results to an appendix, check whether the main conclusions survive under the RBC intervals.** Conventional intervals are narrower because they omit the uncertainty of the bias estimate, not because they are more precise, so this choice overstates significance.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** "As explained above, these bandwidth choices are not valid when conventional distributional approximations are used, but they are fully compatible with our distributional approach" (p. 2309). The authors locate the problem not in MSE-optimal bandwidths themselves but in pairing them with conventional intervals, and they note that this pairing is standard practice: "Since applied researchers often estimate RD treatment effects using local-linear regressions with MSE-optimal bandwidths and implicitly ignore the asymptotic bias of the estimator, the poor coverage of conventional confidence intervals we highlight potentially affects many RD empirical applications" (p. 2301). Cross-validation selectors likewise "typically lead to 'large' bandwidth choices" (p. 2301), and conventional intervals have correct asymptotic coverage only when the bandwidth is small enough for the bias to vanish (p. 2301), which an ad hoc bandwidth does not guarantee.

**For (b).** Conventional bias correction recenters the interval but keeps the old standard error, so "it does not account for the additional variability introduced by the bias estimates" (p. 2302). The asymptotic condition that would justify ignoring this variability never holds in practice: "However, h_n/b_n is never zero in finite samples" (p. 2302). A bias-corrected estimate paired with a conventional standard error therefore still yields an interval that is too narrow.

**For (c).** The authors define the sense in which their intervals are robust: "they provide a robust (to the choice of bandwidths) alternative when compared to the conventional confidence intervals routinely employed in empirical work" (p. 2318). Their robust variance adds a term that "may be interpreted as a correction to account for the variability of the estimated bias-correction term" (p. 2303). It follows that a heteroskedasticity-robust standard error, which contains no such term and involves no bias correction, is not the robust inference the paper proposes.

**For (d).** "However, because the proportionality constant is larger for [the robust interval] than for [the conventional interval], the interval [conventional] may be shorter than [robust] in small samples" (p. 2304, Remark 5). The authors present the extra length as the price of correct coverage: their simulations show "important improvements in empirical coverage (EC) with moderate increments in average empirical interval length (IL)" (p. 2315). A shorter conventional interval is thus bought by giving up coverage.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** RD studies estimated with local polynomial methods whose substantive claims rest on confidence intervals or p-values for the treatment effect at the cutoff. This includes sharp, fuzzy, and kink designs (p. 2305) and falsification tests that use pretreatment or placebo covariates as outcomes (p. 2297). Check (a) applies when conventional inference is paired with an MSE-optimal, cross-validation, or ad hoc bandwidth that is not shown to be small. Check (b) applies only when the manuscript reports "bias-corrected" results, and (c) only when it calls its inference "robust" without saying robust to what. Check (d) applies when RBC results appear only in an appendix or when conventional results carry the main conclusions.

**Does not directly apply to:** discrete running variables. The authors state that when the running variable has few mass points near the cutoff, their results do not apply directly without further assumptions (Remark 1, p. 2299). Age measured in years and school grade are examples.

**Does not apply to:** global polynomial RD, local randomization approaches to RD inference, or identification problems such as manipulation of the running variable or violations of the exclusion restriction. Nor does it apply to MSE-optimal bandwidths paired with RBC intervals: the authors state that these bandwidths are "not valid when conventional distributional approximations are used, but they are fully compatible with our distributional approach" (p. 2309). This is the sharpest boundary in the paper. A check that triggers merely because a manuscript uses an MSE-optimal bandwidth reads the paper backwards.

**Partial application:** fuzzy RD designs with a weak first stage. RBC intervals extend to the fuzzy RD ratio estimator (Theorem 3), but the authors point to separate work for weak-IV-robust inference in fuzzy designs (p. 2297). Flag the concern; do not assert that RBC intervals resolve it.

## 5. What failure looks like

A manuscript estimates a local polynomial RD with an MSE-optimal, cross-validation, or ad hoc bandwidth and rests its claims on conventional confidence intervals and p-values alone. It reports no RBC results; its only safeguard is a sensitivity analysis that re-estimates the effect at half and double the bandwidth, but every one of those estimates uses the same conventional inference and repeats the same bias problem. If the manuscript reports "robust" or "bias-corrected" results, it does not make clear whether "robust" refers to the choice of bandwidth (heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors do not correct the bias) or whether the standard error of the bias-corrected estimate differs from the conventional one (if it is identical, the variability of the bias estimate has been ignored). If RBC results appear only in an appendix, the manuscript does not discuss whether its main conclusions hold under them. If a result is significant only under the conventional interval, a reviewer should note that its significance may be overstated because of the remaining bias, not infer that the effect is zero because the RBC interval is not significant.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Ludwig, Jens, and Douglas L. Miller. 2007. "Does Head Start Improve Children's Life Chances? Evidence from a Regression Discontinuity Design." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 122(1): 159–208. In a local linear RD with the 1960 county poverty rate as the running variable (p. 170), the bandwidth is set to an ad hoc range of 9 to 36 (pp. 170–171) and inference uses conventional analytic standard errors from Porter (2003) (p. 170), so check (a) should fire. Its supplementary bootstrap p-values (p. 170) resample the conventional t-statistic, which Remark 6 (p. 2304) says does not fix the bias.

**Should NOT trigger:** Klašnja, Marko, and Rocío Titiunik. 2017. "The Incumbency Curse: Weak Parties, Term Limits, and Unfulfilled Accountability." *American Political Science Review* 111(1): 129–48. It uses an MSE-optimal bandwidth for local linear RD, but reports CCT's robust confidence intervals and p-values precisely because that bandwidth is too large for conventional intervals (p. 135; Table 2, p. 136). With an MSE-optimal bandwidth, the word "robust," and a fourth-order polynomial RD plot, it carries many surface cues, which makes it a near miss on which no check should fire.
