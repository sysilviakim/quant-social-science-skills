# Identification and Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Mediation Effects

**Contributor:** Hojin Jang

**Full citation:** Imai, Kosuke, Luke Keele, and Teppei Yamamoto. 2010. "Identification, Inference and Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Mediation Effects." *Statistical Science* 25(1): 51–71. doi:10.1214/10-STS321.

## 1. What the paper establishes

The paper asks when mediation estimates from Baron and Kenny's (1986) linear structural equation model (LSEM) are causal. It identifies the average causal mediation effect (ACME) nonparametrically under a sequential ignorability assumption that excludes post-treatment confounders but permits treatment-mediator interaction, and shows that the LSEM's product of coefficients equals the ACME only with added linearity and equal mediation effects across treatment arms. Because randomizing the treatment cannot make the mediator ignorable, the paper adds a sensitivity analysis, and in the authors' framing experiment the result survives large confounding only under the LSEM.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a)** If only the treatment is randomized, check whether the manuscript argues that the mediator is as good as randomly assigned within treatment arms and adjusts both models for the likeliest measured pre-treatment confounder. If the treatment could move a post-treatment variable, such as a second mediator or a post-test control, that also affects the mediator and outcome, dropping it does not help: the authors must rule it out or state the extra assumption that controlling for it requires. A mediator measured after the outcome needs an argument that the outcome items did not change it.

**(b)** Check whether the manuscript decides mediation by Baron and Kenny's causal steps instead of estimating the ACME with a confidence interval. A nonsignificant total effect is no reason to skip the analysis, and with a treatment-mediator interaction, single-coefficient tests cannot show whether the ACME is zero.

**(c)** If one indirect effect comes from a product of coefficients, check whether the manuscript tested a treatment-mediator interaction and, if it allowed one, reported the mediation effect separately under treatment and control. The standard linear model forces the two to be equal, which identification does not require.

**(d)** Check whether the manuscript reports how much unmeasured pre-treatment confounding, as an error correlation or variance explained, would bring the ACME to zero. It should give this threshold for the point estimate and for its confidence interval, separately for confounders that move the mediator and outcome the same way or in opposite ways. Robustness shown under the linear model holds only for that model.

**(e)** Check which way the most plausible pre-treatment confounder would bias the ACME. With a randomized treatment and linear models, the confounder pushes the mediator-outcome coefficient up if it moves both variables the same way and down if it moves them in opposite directions. A push toward the coefficient's estimated sign biases the ACME away from zero, so it may be overstated; a push against that sign makes the estimate conservative.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** Randomization secures only half of the assumption: "the ignorability of the treatment assignment holds but the ignorability of the mediator may not" (p. 52). Measured pre-treatment covariates can enter each regression (p. 57). After conditioning on a post-treatment confounder, identification is "not possible without an additional assumption" (p. 55), such as Robins's no-interaction assumption (p. 56). The mediator must be realized between treatment and outcome (p. 54), yet the authors' own example measured the outcome right after the newscast and the mediators after the experiment (p. 53).

**For (b).** Baron and Kenny's first step requires a significant total effect (p. 57), yet "the ACME can be nonzero even when the average total causal effect is zero" (p. 57). In the authors' reanalysis, the 95% interval for the total effect, $[-1.206, 0.126]$, includes zero, but the interval for the ACME, $[-0.969, -0.051]$, does not, because the total effect is estimated less precisely (Table 3, p. 63). With an interaction term the ACME is $\beta_2(\gamma + t\kappa)$, so testing $\gamma$ or $\kappa$ alone cannot settle whether it is zero (p. 58).

**For (c).** "Assumption 2 is not required for the identification of the ACME under the LSEM" (p. 57). That assumption, equal mediation effects in the two arms, is built into the standard LSEM (p. 57), and an interaction term yields one effect per arm (p. 58). In the framing data the parametric estimates are $-0.566$ (free speech frame) and $-0.451$ (public order frame), the nonparametric interval for the latter includes zero, and the authors impose equality only after a test fails to reject it ($p = 0.238$; p. 63).

**For (d).** Mediator ignorability cannot be tested, and without it the data cannot fix even the sign of the ACME (pp. 62, 68), so the authors require a sensitivity analysis on the error correlation $\rho$ or on variance explained (pp. 52, 61–62). In the framing data the estimate stays negative unless $\rho < -0.68$, but "the confidence interval covers the value of zero only when $-0.79 < \rho < -0.49$" (p. 64). The thresholds also depend on direction: a confounder that moves both variables the same way never flips the sign, while one with opposite effects would need to explain at least 27.7% of the residual variance in both (p. 64). A nonparametric analysis looked much less robust, though it is parameterized and coded differently (p. 69).

**For (e).** Under the LSEM, $\rho$ has the sign of the product of the confounder's effects on the mediator and the outcome (p. 62). The proof of Theorem 4 gives $\tilde{\rho}\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \gamma\sigma_2^2 + \rho\sigma_2\sigma_3$ (p. 67), so the coefficient estimated under $\rho = 0$ exceeds the true $\gamma$ by $\rho\sigma_3/\sigma_2$, which has the sign of $\rho$. Because the treatment raised public order importance (p. 53) and the ACME is negative (p. 63), that coefficient is negative in the framing study. The authors expect that "conservatism is likely to be positively correlated with both public order importance and tolerance for the Klan" (p. 64); such a confounder pushes the coefficient toward zero, which makes the estimate conservative.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** manuscripts that report a mediation effect (an ACME, an indirect effect, a Sobel test, a proportion mediated, or a conditional indirect effect) for a measured rather than manipulated mediator. Checks (a) and (d) run on all of them; with an observational treatment, (a) also covers treatment ignorability, which the $\rho$ analysis assumes (p. 61). The post-treatment part of (a) runs when a post-treatment variable enters the models or several mediators are analyzed, one at a time or in parallel. (b) runs only when a significant total effect is made a precondition or mediation is inferred from individual coefficients; a Sobel test of the product does not trigger it, since it tests the ACME under the LSEM (p. 58). (c) needs a single indirect effect from a linear product of coefficients, and an interaction of the treatment with a pre-treatment covariate or a second randomized factor neither triggers nor satisfies it. (e) stays silent unless the treatment is randomized and the models are linear, because its sign rule needs an unbiased treatment-mediator effect (p. 61).

**Partial application:** several causally related mediators, or a mismeasured mediator. Run the checks, but flag that the authors leave both problems to future research (p. 66), without claiming the paper's results cover them. A $\rho$ analysis leaves the post-treatment part of (a) open (p. 61). A binary mediator or outcome is in scope, since Theorem 1 covers discrete and continuous mediators alike (p. 56); (d) should then ask for the probit version (p. 62) or the bounds analysis in Appendix E (pp. 68–69), and the linear-product logic of (c) and (e) does not carry over.

**Does not apply to:** pure moderation analyses with no indirect effect, controlled direct effects, and manipulation checks that only confirm the treatment was received; moderated mediation stays in scope. The paper defines controlled direct effects at fixed mediator values and treats them as a separate estimand (p. 55), so the post-treatment part of (a) stays silent for them, even when they condition on post-treatment variables. A manipulation check offered as evidence of the mechanism, though, triggers (a) and (b), since it shows only the treatment's effect on the mediator (p. 58).

## 5. What failure looks like

A paper reports an indirect effect for a measured mediator, relies on randomization of the treatment alone, and has no sensitivity analysis or plot of the ACME against $\rho$. It lets one indirect effect stand for both conditions without testing a treatment-mediator interaction, lists post-test items among its covariates, or enters several mediators without asking how they affect each other. A sentence may say that mediation was not tested because the treatment effect was not significant. If the likeliest confounder pushes the mediator-outcome coefficient toward its estimated sign, the reviewer should note that the ACME may be overstated, not conclude that the mechanism is absent.

## 6. Test cases

**Should Trigger:** Feinholdt, Alina, Andreas R. T. Schuck, Sophie K. Lecheler, and Claes H. de Vreese. 2017. "Shifting Frames: Conditional Indirect Effects of Contested Issues on Perceived Effectiveness Through Multiple Emotions." *Journal of Media Psychology* 29(2): 81–91. The authors randomize issue contestation and frame (pp. 84–85) and report parallel PROCESS indirect effects on perceived policy effectiveness (PPE) through anger, fear, and hope (p. 86). Both models control for a post-test item, perceived temporal distance, that began as part of the manipulation and marginally affected a mediator (p. 85, n. 5; p. 86), and no sensitivity analysis is reported, so (a) and (d) run. (c) runs because no treatment-mediator interaction is tested; the contestation $\times$ frame interaction (p. 88) is not one. Under (e), favorable prior views of the policy, not measured at baseline (p. 89), would likely raise both hope and PPE, and the hope coefficient is positive ($b = .39$, p. 86), so that indirect effect may be overstated. (b) stays silent: mediation is tested even where contestation had no significant effect on PPE (pp. 86–87).

**Should NOT Trigger:** Acharya, Avidit, Matthew Blackwell, and Maya Sen. 2016. "The Political Legacy of American Slavery." *The Journal of Politics* 78(3): 621–641. A near-miss: testing whether slavery's effect on white attitudes runs through today's Black population share, the authors add that "mediator" as a control (p. 628) and condition on intermediate covariates (p. 630, n. 14), which may tempt an agent to fire (a) or (b). But they flag those first estimates as open to post-treatment bias, drop mediation analysis because of intermediate confounding, and estimate a controlled direct effect by sequential g-estimation (p. 629), an estimand that Imai, Keele, and Yamamoto keep apart from the ACME (p. 55). No indirect effect is reported, so no check applies, and (e) is out of scope because slavery was not randomly assigned.
