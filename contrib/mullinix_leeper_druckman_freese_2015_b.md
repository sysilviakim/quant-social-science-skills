## <The Generalizability of Survey Experiments>

**Contributor:** Daniel Seungwon Kang

**Full citation:** <Mullinix, Kevin J., Thomas J. Leeper, James N. Druckman, and Jeremy Freese. 2015. "The Generalizability of Survey Experiments." Journal of Experimental Political Science 2 (2): 109–38.>



## 1. What the paper establishes:
The paper establishes that convenience samples generally produce treatment effects that are similar to population samples. Although convenience samples were conventionally thought to produce biased results due to their limited scope, comparing population-sample estimates from TESS with various convenience sample estimates shows that convenience samples can serve as useful testing grounds for exploring an experimental query. 



## 2. Checks for a reviewer:

(a) \textbf{Check whether a cross-sample comparison changed more than sample composition} Ask whether recruitment, survey mode, setting, or compensation differ across the collection and implementation of experiments on samples. The paper cautions that "the samples differ in more than just their composition," (p. 116) which limits the experiments' ability to infer whether composition alone explains similarities or differences in treatment effects. When the procedure differs, the study should acknowledge its conclusion as a comparison of the combined sample and implementation conditions. 

(b) \textbf{Check whether the study justifies its weighting choice} If a study generalizes results from a convenience sample to a population, check whether it justifies its weighting choice and reports whether reasonable weighting adjustments change the estimated treatment effects (p.122).

(c) \textbf{Check whether the sample has enough variation in moderators supported by theory} If a study predicts heterogeneous treatment effects, inspect whether its sample includes adequate coverage of the proposed moderator (p.123). This check applies mostly to studies making subgroup claims; it should stay silent when the paper makes only an average-effect claim and does not posit heterogeneity. 

(d) \textbf{Check whether the evidence about a changing convenience sample is adequately collected for the claim} Ask whether changes in the sample over time could affect who participates or how they respond, and whether the study provides current evidence that the sample can support its conclusions (p.124). 



## 3. Evidence:

For (a). The authors caution that Study 1's cross-convenience sample comparisons do not isolate sample composition from differences in experiment implementation and their results. They write that "the samples differ in more than just their comparison" (p.116). This supports a needed check of whether a study varies the methods of collecting and implementing experiments with different samples. 

For (b). The authors find "decidedly mixed" results after weighting MTurk data. While the paper's scope did not include weighting in the main model since it is unconventional to do so, the authors also acknowledge the need of research in understanding consequences of weighted adjustments (p.122). 

For (c). The authors explain that experiments often have heterogeneous effects, which should be moderated with a well-developed theory of heterogeneity. Examples the authors provide for inappropriate convenience samples even with an appropriate theory are student samples where a moderator is age, and university staff sample where moderator is education (p.123). 

For (d). The authors mention that the nature of convenience samples changes over time, and convenience samples such as MTurk should be monitored in terms of reliability for replicating population-based experimental inferences (p.124). Thus, when a study makes a claim about a target population, evidence from an earlier version of a changing sample pool may not establish that the current pool can answer the question.



## 4. Scope:

\textbf{Applies to:} Survey experiments that use convenience samples to make claims about a target population. Check (a) applies when sample type is compared and the manuscript attributes differences in effects to sample composition; it is especially relevant when samples also differ in mode, setting, or compensation (p. 116). Check (b) applies when a paper uses weighting—or omits it—to support a population-generalizability claim (pp. 115, 122). Check (c) applies when the paper theorizes or estimates heterogeneous treatment effects and relies on a sample to represent the relevant moderator values (p. 123). Check (d) applies when a manuscript relies on evidence about a changing convenience sample, such as MTurk, to establish that the current sample can answer its research question (p. 124).

\textbf{Does not apply to:} studies that use a single sample and make no claim about how its treatment effects generalize across samples or to a target population. These checks also do not assess whether a study’s cross-sectional or before-and-after design identifies a causal effect; they concern whether its sample comparisons and generalization claims are supported. A paper’s use of an in-person or paper survey does not by itself put it outside scope: Study 1 included samples administered in person and by paper, and the authors discuss those implementation differences as a limit on attributing results to sample composition (pp. 114–116).



## 5. What failure looks like:
A study compares treatment effects from multiple convenience samples and attributes any similarities or differences to sample composition, but the samples differ in survey mode, setting, or compensation, etc. It reports that the multiple estimates without acknowledging that these implementation differences could also explain the pattern.

A study makes a population-generalizability claim from an unweighted convenience sample without explaining the choice or how weighting could affect the estimated treated effects. 

A study claims that treatment effects vary across subgroups, but its convenience sample includes little or not variation in the proposed moderator. 

A study uses a result of treatment effects from an earlier convenience group to answer current issues without providing evidence that the pool may have changed over time. 




## 6. Test cases:

\textbf{SHOULD trigger:} Nachman, Lev, Nathan Kar Ming Chan, and Chit Wai John Mok. “Desperate Acts of Democracy: Identity Threat, Autocratization and Voter Turnout in Hong Kong.” \textit {The China Quarterly} 266 (2026): 453–69. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305741026102288. This paper conducts and compares three different periods and reports different treatment effects, of which the first two follow the exact two procedures for sample collection, and the third is a comparable prime that tests for robustness (p.459). This checks (a) as it justifies for the different methods for sample collection and including them in the model. For check (b) while the paper does not explicitly mention weighing, it discusses the possibility of the small sample size on its estimated effects (p.462 footnote 47). Check (c) should be triggered as the study hypothesizes different effects between Hong Konger and Chinese identifying respondents (p.459). Check (d) is also validated since the paper acknowledged the use of a sample according to a particular time (p.459).

\textbf{Should NOT trigger:} Kao, Jay C. 2026. "How the Pro-Beijing Media Influences Voters: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment." \textit{American Journal of Political Sciences}. 120(3): 795-814. The study describes one field experiment, not a comparison of different treatment effects across samples (799-800). The experiment also studies one election-period field experiment and does not rely on earlier validation (p.795). There is a heterogeneity report based on non-partisan, PRC-friendly, and PRC-skeptical voters (p.795), which is included in the sample. However, since the baseline design starts from a single field experiment, not cross-sample comparisons, this should not be triggered.
