# A More Credible Approach to Parallel Trends

**Full citation:** Rambachan, Ashesh, and Jonathan Roth. 2023. "A More Credible Approach to Parallel Trends." *The Review of Economic Studies* 90(5): 2555–2591.

**Contributor:** Kwon Seong Jun

---

## 1. What the paper establishes

A difference-in-differences (DiD) study assumes parallel trends which means without the treatment, the treated group and the comparison group would have changed in the same way. Researchers usually defend this assumption by showing that the pre-treatment coefficients in an event-study plot (the "leads") are not statistically significant. However Rambachan and Roth show that this test is weak, and that passing it does not make the usual confidence interval safe.They propose different approach. They state how much larger the post-treatment violation of parallel trends could be compared with what is seen before treatment. Under that assumption the treatment effect is a range, not a single number, and the paper gives confidence sets for it. Repeating this with weaker assumptions illustrates the point where the conclusion stops holding.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether the paper uses "no significant pre-trend" as proof of parallel trends.** Look at the event-study figure. If the confidence intervals of the leads are wide compared with the standard error of the main estimate, the test could miss a violation that is big enough to matter. Also check whether a specification seems to have been chosen because its leads looked clean.

**(b) If the paper reports a sensitivity analysis of this kind, check that it says which restriction it uses and why.** The paper has two main restrictions. One compares the size of post-treatment violations with pre-treatment ones (relative magnitudes, with parameter M̄). The other limits how fast the trend can bend (smoothness, with parameter M). "Robust up to M = 1" means nothing until the reader knows which one is used, what the units are, and why it fits the setting.

**(c) Check that the sensitivity analysis is about the same effect that the paper claims.** Later-period effects and averages over many periods are usually less robust than the first post-treatment effect. A paper that claims a long-run effect but tests only the first period is overstating its robustness.

**(d) Check how the paper handles a pre-trend that clearly slopes.** Two common responses are adding a group-specific linear trend, or arguing that the pre-trend goes in the "wrong direction." A linear trend is roughly the special case M = 0, so it is one assumption, not a sensitivity analysis. Ask what happens to the result when M is allowed to be larger than zero.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors states "pre-trends tests may be underpowered against meaningful violations of parallel trends, potentially leading to severe undercoverage of conventional confidence intervals" (p. 2559). They add that "conditioning the analysis on passing pre-trends tests introduces statistical issues related to pre-testing" (p. 2555). There is also a footnote saying that if the worst-case bias is b standard errors, a 95% confidence interval really covers the truth with probability "0.95 for b = 0, 0.83 for b = 1, 0.48 for b = 2" (p. 2580, n. 31). So a bias of only one or two standard errors already matters, and this is why the check compares the leads' intervals with the main standard error.

**For (b).** The paper says "The choice of Δ should be motivated by economic knowledge about the types of possible confounding factors that could produce non-parallel trends" (p. 2582). Here Δ means the set of violations the researcher allows. The relative-magnitudes restriction limits post-treatment violations to "̄M bar times the maximum pre-treatment violation" (p. 2563). The smoothness restriction limits how much "the slope of δ can change between consecutive periods" (p. 2564). The two applications in the paper choose differently. In case of a restaurant tax cut, the worry is industry shocks, so the authors use relative magnitudes (pp. 2583–2584). For teacher bargaining laws, the worry is slow long-run trends, so they use smoothness (p. 2586). They also explain the size of M in real units (p. 2587).

**For (c).** The authors recommend reporting the parameter value "at which particular hypotheses of interest can no longer be rejected" (p. 2583). In the tax application with ̄M bar = 1, the confidence set for the first-year effect is [0.07, 0.31], which does not include zero. But the set for the four-year average "now includes zero, and is about twice as large as for the first-period effect" (p. 2584). The authors explain why: "the identified set will be larger for later periods, since the treatment and control groups have more time to diverge" (p. 2584). The same data and the same assumption give a different answer depending on which effect is tested.

**For (d).** When M = 0, the smoothness restriction "requires that the difference in trends be exactly linear, which corresponds with the assumption underlying the parametric linear specification common in applied work" (p. 2564). The authors warn that this kind of control "may be sensitive to functional form assumptions" (p. 2559). They also note that direction arguments are common, quoting one study's claim that its pre-trends are "either zero or in the wrong direction" (p. 2564; look at the footnote number 10). The teacher-bargaining application shows the danger. The original estimate for women is negative and significant. But with a linear extrapolation of the pre-trend, "for M < 0.01 the robust confidence set contains only positive values" (p. 2587). The sign of the result flips. Once M is about 0.01 or larger, a zero effect cannot be ruled out for men or women (p. 2587).

## 4. Scope

**(a) Applies to:** observational DiD or event-study papers that show leads or a pre-trend test to support parallel trends. **Does not apply to:** randomized experiments, and designs with only two time periods, because there are no leads to look at. A significant lead is also not an automatic failure. In the tax application the pre-trend test rejects, but the first-year effect is still robust at M̄ = 1 (p. 2584).

**(b) Applies to:** papers that report this kind of sensitivity analysis or claim robustness to violations of parallel trends. **Does not apply to:** papers that do neither. The reviewer should not demand one restriction for every paper. The authors themselves say a smooth trend would be "unreasonable" for industry shocks (p. 2584), and they allow other restrictions such as sign and monotonicity (pp. 2564–2565). The reviewer also should not ask authors to estimate M̄ or M from the data, because the restriction "must be specified by the researcher" (p. 2563). M̄ = 1 is a natural benchmark only "When the number of pre-treatment and post-treatment periods is similar" (p. 2563).

**(c) Applies to:** papers that report a sensitivity analysis, or where the reviewer asks for one under (a) or (d). **Does not apply to:** papers whose claim is only about the first post-treatment period. The idea that later effects are less robust holds for restrictions on period-to-period changes, but it "need not necessarily hold for other types of restrictions" (p. 2584, footnote number 34). A breakdown value is also not a measure of how large the violation really is. It only shows what has to be assumed.

**(d) Applies to:** papers that add group-specific linear trends, remove a pre-period slope, or argue from the direction of a pre-trend. **Does not apply to:** papers whose leads are flat and that make no trend adjustment. It also does not fit settings where the problem is sudden shocks, not slow trends, as in the tax application (p. 2584). In that case (b) is the right check. A direction argument is not wrong in itself. The paper shows it can be written as a formal monotonicity restriction (p. 2564), and the request is to do that and report the confidence set.

## 5. What failure looks like

The event-study figure shows leads with wide confidence intervals, and the text says none of the pre-treatment coefficients is significant, which supports parallel trends. The paper then relies on the ordinary 95% interval and says nothing more about the assumption. A robustness table answers a sloped pre-trend with one line which states that results are robust to state-specific linear trends. The paper reports "HonestDiD, M = 1" but does not say which restriction it used, what one unit of M means, or why that value is reasonable.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Albertus, Michael, and Victor Gay. 2026. "State-Building and Rebellion in the Run-Up to the French Revolution." American Political Science Review 120(3): 1031–1058. This is a staggered DiD study of new horse-post relays and rebellions in French parishes. (a) should run. The authors say parallel trends is "fundamentally untestable" (p. 1042), but then write that the placebo leads "show no evidence of differential pre-trends" (p. 1045) and take this as support for the assumption. They focus on two leads because the third is based on few switchers (pp. 1044–1045). (c) should also run. The estimated effects "continue to grow thereafter" (p. 1045), so the paper's argument depends on later-decade effects, and any sensitivity analysis requested under (a) should cover those. (b) and (d) should stay silent unless the robustness section or supplement reports a sensitivity analysis or a trend adjustment. Triggering means the checks should be run. It does not mean the study is wrong.

**Should NOT trigger:** Kao, Jay C. 2026. "How the Pro-Beijing Media Influences Voters: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment." American Political Science Review 120(3): 795–814. This is a near-miss. The study has a baseline survey before the treatment (p. 799) and an endline survey after it (p. 800), and the author writes "I analyze outcomes as change scores rather than endline values" (p. 805). Comparing before-and-after changes between a treated group and a control group looks like DiD. But participants were assigned to conditions "using blocked randomization by partisanship" (p. 799). Because of randomization, the groups are comparable by design, so the study does not need a parallel trends assumption. There are also only two survey waves, so there are no leads. All four checks should stay silent.
