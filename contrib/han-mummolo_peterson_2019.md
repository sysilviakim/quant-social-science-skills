# Demand Effects in Survey Experiments: An Empirical Assessment

**Full citation:** Mummolo, Jonathan, and Erik Peterson. 2019. "Demand Effects in Survey Experiments: An Empirical Assessment." *American Political Science Review* 113(2): 517–529. doi:10.1017/S0003055418000837.

---

## 1. What the paper establishes

Experimenter demand effects (EDEs) arise when participants infer a researcher's hypothesis and respond in ways intended to help confirm it, potentially biasing an experimental treatment effect. Despite frequent concern about EDEs, their prevalence and consequences in survey experiments had received little direct empirical testing. Mummolo and Peterson replicate five existing experimental designs and, independently of each original treatment, randomize the information respondents receive about the experimenter's hypothesis.

The authors first check whether this information makes respondents more likely to identify the hypothesis. They then test whether revealing it changes the original experiments' treatment effects. A directional design gives different respondents opposing expectations about the sign of an effect; an incentive design offers a bonus for answers that support the stated hypothesis. Revealing the hypothesis generally increases awareness without increasing treatment effects. Financial incentives produce some exceptions, especially in Mechanical Turk samples, but no consistent demand effects across the experiments. The authors conclude that EDEs may be less consequential for the online survey experiments they study than is often assumed, while allowing that some topics, respondent pools, or very small effects may be more vulnerable.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) If a survey experiment hides its treatment, outcome measure, or purpose to prevent EDEs, check whether the paper specifies the expected path from hypothesis awareness to biased responses and explains how the precaution addresses it.**

A cover story, disguised treatment, or deception should have a stated purpose in the particular design. The reviewer should ask which respondents could infer the researcher's expectations, how they could alter their answers, and whether the proposed precaution would interrupt that process. This is a request for a design-specific rationale, not a presumption that concealment is always unnecessary.

**(b) If a paper predicts that EDEs differ across respondent pools or subgroups, check whether the prediction identifies which part of the demand process should vary and is reflected in the design and analysis.**

Experienced survey takers may be more alert to researcher expectations, yet awareness does not imply willingness to help confirm a hypothesis. A claim about greater EDE risk should distinguish the ability to infer a hypothesis from the inclination and ability to answer in line with it.

**(c) If respondents might answer in the hypothesized direction even without learning the hypothesis, check whether the EDE study can distinguish that sincere response from one induced by researcher expectations.**

When respondents' preexisting preferences already favor the outcome a researcher expects, sincere and EDE-driven answers can point in the same direction. Randomly assigning opposite expectations about the direction of the treatment effect, as in the paper's directional scheme, tests whether responses shift with the stated expectation.

**(d) Before accepting a reported absence of EDEs, check whether the hypothesis-information treatment actually increased respondents' awareness of the hypothesis; then examine evidence about their ability and inclination to comply.**

A null result could mean that respondents did not understand the hypothesis, could not produce the requested pattern of answers, or did not want to do so. A hypothesis-guessing manipulation check and an incentive condition help distinguish these explanations. If the paper compares treatment effects across respondents who guessed a hypothesis correctly or incorrectly, check that it does not form these groups using a post-treatment response from the same experiment.

**(e) If a paper argues that EDEs are generally limited, check whether it tested conditions that should make them easier to detect: experienced survey takers, simple treatment contrasts, explicit information about the hypothesis, and incentives to confirm it; and whether exceptions or differences across samples narrow the conclusion.**

Each condition addresses a different obstacle to EDEs: respondents must notice the researcher's expectation, understand how to answer in line with it, and have a reason to do so. The reviewer should identify which obstacles the design actually addresses and assess the claim against any effects consistent with EDEs. These features describe this paper's test; they are not a checklist that every study must adopt.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors say precautions against EDEs rest on the assumption that demand effects can be limited by "obfuscating an experimenter's intentions" (p. 518). In their conclusion, they suggest that substantial resources spent concealing hypotheses to prevent demand-like behavior "may be misguided" (p. 528). They do not show that every form of concealment is pointless: they explicitly note other reasons to mask a study's purpose, including reducing social desirability bias (p. 528; see also p. 517, n. 1). A reviewer therefore needs the paper's own account of the EDE mechanism and of what the precaution is meant to accomplish.

**For (b).** The authors choose Mechanical Turk partly because prior work portrays it as "a particularly likely case for demand effects to occur" given its labor-market setting (p. 520). They also warn that, even if participants infer researcher intent, they "may not be inclined to assist researchers" (p. 519). Their comparisons across Mechanical Turk and Qualtrics samples show why survey experience, hypothesis awareness, and hypothesis-confirming behavior should be treated as distinct steps (pp. 520, 525).

**For (c).** In the gradation scheme, respondents might answer in the original hypothesis's direction even without knowing it; the same answers after learning the hypothesis could be mistaken for demand. In the directional scheme, the authors randomly provide an expectation of either a positive or a negative treatment effect. As they put it, "The directional design breaks this observational equivalence" (p. 521). A reviewer should look for a comparable way to distinguish the two explanations when an EDE study attributes a response to demand.

**For (d).** The authors call it a "first-order concern" to verify that respondents understood what the demand treatments revealed (p. 522). They ask respondents to identify the experiment's hypothesis and find that the information treatments increased correct guesses (pp. 522–523, Figure 1; p. 525, Figure 3). They then distinguish being unable to generate EDEs from being able but unwilling, using a bonus for hypothesis-confirming answers to probe that distinction (p. 524). For their subgroup comparison, they classify respondents by whether they guessed the hypothesis of a *previous* experiment, then compare randomized treatment effects in the next experiment; note 9 explicitly says this avoids the post-treatment bias that a within-experiment comparison would create (p. 527).

**For (e).** The test has several concrete features intended to expose EDEs. Mechanical Turk respondents have substantial survey experience and may be especially attentive to researcher expectations (p. 520). The replicated experiments have one treatment and one control condition and make little effort to disguise researcher intent (p. 525). The authors then reveal the hypothesis and, in some conditions, offer a bonus for answering in a way that confirms it (pp. 518, 524–525). Revealing the hypothesis alone does not generally change treatment effects. With a financial incentive, however, the Mechanical Turk democratic-peace and welfare experiments show increases consistent with EDEs, while the Qualtrics experiments do not (p. 525, Figure 4). The conclusion recognizes possible variation by topic and subject pool and some risk for studies seeking very small effects (pp. 528–529). A broad claim that EDEs never occur would exceed this evidence.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** self-administered survey experiments in which respondents encounter both the experimental treatment and outcome measures in a survey, and the paper treats EDEs as a design or interpretation problem. This includes studies that invoke EDEs to justify concealment, investigate demand effects directly, or interpret a treatment effect as robust to them. The paper's direct empirical evidence is mainly from online survey experiments; it does not establish the same result for every respondent pool or subject matter (pp. 518–519, 528).

The checks are conditional on the claim being reviewed. Check (a) applies when concealment is justified as an EDE precaution; (b) when differences across respondent types are predicted or interpreted; (c) when an EDE analysis cannot otherwise separate demand from sincere preferences; (d) when the paper reports no EDEs or explains that null result; and (e) when it generalizes from tests designed to increase awareness of, or incentives to confirm, a researcher's hypothesis. These are not blanket requirements for every survey experiment.

**Does not directly apply to:** laboratory experiments with direct researcher–participant interaction, field experiments measuring behavior outside the survey, or behavioral games where incentives and behavioral costs are part of the outcome itself. Demand effects may occur in those settings, but the cues available to participants and the costs of acting on them differ from those in the self-administered survey setting analyzed here (pp. 518–519).

**Does not apply to:** observational surveys without an experimental treatment effect, or ordinary survey experiments that make no claim about EDEs and use no EDE-based design precaution. Such studies do not automatically need a hypothesis-guessing check, opposing directional hypotheses, or an incentive condition.

**Partial application:** even if a paper does not explicitly analyze EDEs, a survey that tells respondents what answer the researcher expects or rewards answers in a specified direction presents a concrete demand pathway worth examining. This alone does not justify requiring every additional design feature in checks (b)–(e). Social desirability bias calls for these EDE-specific checks only when the paper also posits that respondents answer to satisfy the researcher rather than merely to appear socially acceptable (p. 517, n. 1).

## 5. What failure looks like

A survey experiment says only that it concealed its purpose "to prevent demand effects." It does not explain how respondents might infer the hypothesis, which answers would confirm it, or how those answers would bias the reported treatment effect. It also treats attentive respondents and respondents willing to help the researcher as the same group, without evidence for that link.

In a direct EDE study, a response in the predicted direction is labeled demand-driven even though it could express a sincere preference. Or the paper reports no EDEs without showing that respondents learned the hypothesis, then attributes the null result to unwillingness without considering inability to produce the expected answer. Finally, it generalizes from a limited set of online experiments to all survey experiments despite incentive-condition exceptions and possible variation by topic or respondent pool.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** DeMora, Stephanie L., Jennifer L. Merolla, Brian Newman, and Elizabeth J. Zechmeister. 2024. "Jesus was a Refugee: Religious Values Framing can Increase Support for Refugees Among White Evangelical Republicans." *Political Behavior* 46: 2145–2168. doi:10.1007/s11109-024-09912-2.

The authors explicitly say they "intentionally mask the treatment to minimize experimenter demand effects" (p. 2153), placing the frames unobtrusively in an opinion module. Check (a) therefore applies: a reviewer can ask what EDE pathway that placement is supposed to block and how the design supports that rationale. This trigger is a reason to examine the explanation, not evidence that the study is flawed.

**Should NOT trigger:** Ishizuka, Patrick. 2019. "Social Class, Gender, and Contemporary Parenting Standards in the United States: Evidence from a National Survey Experiment." *Social Forces* 98(1): 31–58. doi:10.1093/sf/soy107.

This vignette experiment examines how social class and gender shape judgments about good parenting. Its use of a survey experiment alone does not trigger the EDE-specific checks above. A reviewer should apply them only if the paper also makes a relevant claim about demand effects or justifies a design choice as an EDE precaution.
