# The Perils of Balance Testing

**Contributor:** Minje Park

**Full citation:** Mutz, Diana C., Robin Pemantle, and Philip Pham. 2019. "The Perils of Balance Testing in Experimental Design: Messy Analyses of Clean Data." The American Statistician 73 (1): 32–42

## 1. What the paper establishes
Balance tests are inappropriate in clean experiments where randomization was carried out, treatments were delivered and there was no differential attrition. Their main danger is Balance Test and Adjust (BT&A), choosing covariates after seeing which are imbalanced, which invalidates the reported confidence levels, makes false positives easier to produce, and mistakes a gain in precision for a gain in credibility. The authors recommend choosing covariates in advance for their expected relation to the outcome and describing the randomization procedure rather than testing it.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer
**(a) Check whether any covariate entered the model because it was imbalanced across conditions (p. 34).** Look for wording such as “included because of imbalances” or “the results were substantially the same” once the imbalanced variable is controlled for (p. 33). If so, ask whether the covariate set was fixed before the data were seen and request the pre-specified estimate (p. 36). The same check applies to post-stratifying on an imbalanced covariate (p. 38).

**(b) When a result reaches significance only after a Balance Test and Adjust (BT&A) adjustment, treat it as weaker evidence, not stronger (p. 41).** A covariate that fails a balance test is unusually likely to push a borderline p-value across 0.05 (p. 40). Imbalance also raises how strongly a covariate must predict the outcome before adjusting for it helps (p. 37). Borderline results are the most at risk while a result that holds under the pre-specified model is not threatened by the imbalance (p. 36). The reviewer who insists on the adjustment “confuses efficiency with credibility” (p. 36).

**(c) Check whether the manuscript treats a balance table or a non-significant balance test as evidence that randomization was “successful” or that internal validity was established (p. 32).** Randomization is shown by the procedure, not by a test of its output (p. 35). Also check whether a baseline table is presented with no stated purpose, since that leaves readers to judge balance subjectively (p. 37).

**(d) Check whether balance results were used to rerandomize, discard the sample, or discount the conclusion as an “unlucky draw” (p. 38).** Rerandomization is valid only under a rejection rule fixed before assignment (p. 39). Screening after the fact removes few false positives and increases Type II error (p. 39).

## 3. Evidence
**For (a).** The authors define Balance Test and Adjust (BT&A) as a procedure in which researchers first choose covariates and then “if the researcher observes any ways in which other pretreatment measures are not balanced across treatment groups, include those variables as well” (p. 34). Its first listed consequence is that “[c]onfidence statements are wrong” (p. 34) because this sequential procedure is a different probability model that “has different confidence levels” (p. 37). Any rule that lets pretreatment measures shape the model “would need to be prespecified if inferences are to remain valid” (p. 36), so “[a]ny covariate that one would be tempted to include should it flunk a balance test, should be included from the start” (p. 38). The same applies to stratification: “a failed balance test is not a good reason to perform a stratified analysis” (p. 38).

**For (b).** In simulations where all potential covariates “are all, in fact, independent of the dependent variable” (p. 40), adding a single covariate raises the nominal 5% Type I error “to between 6% (N = 400) and 8.5% (N = 50),” and “[f]ailing a balance test triples the chance” that the covariate pushes a borderline result across p < 0.05 (p. 40). Theorem 1 implies that “one should be less inclined to include Z, not more, if you see that Z is imbalanced” (p. 37). The authors conclude that covariates chosen after the experiment “should produce greater rather than lesser skepticism about the results” (p. 41). A reviewer who insists on the adjustment “confuses efficiency with credibility” (p. 36). Hence a result that becomes significant only after adjustment is weaker evidence and the adjustment should not be perceived as a condition of credibility.

**For (c).** The authors state that “[a]nyone who talks about balance testing in terms of establishing ‘successful randomization’ has lost track of the statistical model” (p. 35). As for tables, providing one “with no further instruction seems like a decidedly bad idea” (p. 37) as baseline tables “lead to subjective interpretations outside of any statistical model” (p. 41). A balance table therefore cannot confirm randomization, which can only be done by the procedure's description.

**For (d).** The authors note that “[t]he underlying statistical theory contains no information as to when one has encountered one of these ‘unlucky draws’” (p. 34). This is because probability theory “does not provide a definition of randomness for an instance of randomization” (p. 38). They endorse Morgan and Rubin’s rule to rerandomize only “if the decision to rerandomize or not is based on a prespecified criterion” (p. 39). For a covariate correlated 0.3 with the outcome, screening out unlucky draws reduces false positives no more than “testing significance at a 4.5% level instead of a 5% level,” and it “always produces a greater Type II error” (p. 39).

## 4. Scope
**Applies to:** randomized experiments that are clean, meaning randomization was carried out, treatments were delivered, and attrition did not differ by condition (pp. 33–34). **Check (a) applies when** covariates were added or a stratified analysis was run after a balance table or test revealed imbalance, since the model is then chosen after looking at the data (pp. 34, 38). **Check (b) applies when** a result crosses a significance threshold only under the adjusted model, because a covariate that fails a balance test is unusually likely to produce that boost (p. 40). **Check (c) applies when** a manuscript presents a balance table or test as confirming randomization or internal validity, since statistical models “do not differentiate between a ‘good’ or ‘bad’ randomization” (p. 35). **Check (d) applies when** balance results were used after assignment to rerandomize, drop data, or discount a finding as an unlucky draw (p. 38).

**Does not directly apply to:** experiments with botched randomization or differential attrition where balance tests “can be an important tool in determining whether to treat the data as observational versus experimental” (p. 32), or to observational studies where “any imbalanced covariate is a possible explanation for a spurious relationship” (p. 33).

**Check (a) does not apply to** covariates chosen in advance for their expected relation to the outcome, since it is “generally a mistake not to include in the model a variable known or strongly suspected to predict the dependent variable” (p. 36), or to adjustment rules that were pre-specified (p. 36).

**Check (b) does not apply to** results already significant under the pre-specified model, because adding a covariate changes their precision, not their credibility (p. 36). Its specific figures come from simulations with dichotomous covariates independent of the outcome (p. 40) and should not be cited as exact for other designs.

**Check (c) does not apply to** blocking or matching at the design stage, which the authors call optimal when balance on a known set of covariates is the concern (p. 35).

**Check (d) does not apply to** rerandomization under a rejection criterion fixed before assignment (p. 39).

## 5. What failure looks like
A paper reports a baseline table by condition, treats the absence of significant differences as proof that randomization succeeded, and adds whichever covariates looked imbalanced to its main model without any indication. The unadjusted or pre-specified estimate is missing, which leaves the reader unable to see how much the finding owes to the adjustment. The biggest problem arises when significance appears only in the adjusted model and the authors describe that model as the more trustworthy one. A related version discounts the finding or conducts rerandomization because the draw seemed unlucky under no criterion set before assignment. A reviewer should keep in mind that when the pre-specified estimate is significant on its own, the imbalance does not undermine it and requiring the adjustment would treat a gain in precision as if it were a gain in credibility.

## 6. Test cases
**SHOULD trigger:** Scherer, Nancy, and Brett Curry. 2010. “Does Descriptive Race Representation Enhance Institutional Legitimacy? The Case of the U.S. Courts.” The Journal of Politics 72(1): 90–104. The authors performed difference-of-means tests on the demographic composition of subjects in each of three experimental conditions “to ensure that the experimental conditions were randomly distributed—thus establishing the internal validity of our experiment” (p. 95). Finding no significant differences, they conclude that they “need only perform an analysis of variance (ANOVA)” because “the control variables that would be employed in a regression were randomly distributed” (p. 95). Check (c) should fire, because a non-significant balance test is treated as proof that randomization succeeded. Checks (a) and (b) should stay silent, because no covariate is added on the basis of the test. A good agent fires (c) not (a).

**Should NOT trigger:** Morgan, Kari Lock, and Donald B. Rubin. 2012. “Rerandomization to Improve Covariate Balance in Experiments.” Annals of Statistics 40(2): 1263–1282. Page numbers below refer to the arXiv preprint (arXiv:1207.5625v1). The paper looks like a target for check (d) at first. It proposes rerandomization whenever covariate balance between treatment and control is poor, for example when the Mahalanobis distance between the groups exceeds a threshold (p. 17). Still the rule is fixed before assignment: “We only advocate rerandomization if the decision to rerandomize or not is based on a pre-specified criterion” (p. 3). Under such a rule, the procedure is rejection sampling with a well-defined probability model (p. 5) and the stated benefit to this is efficiency as “more powerful tests and narrower confidence intervals” (p. 17). As a result, check (d) should stay silent. Triggering (d) here would mean the agent is reacting to rerandomization itself, not to rerandomization after seeing an imbalance.
