# Elements of External Validity: Framework, Design, and Analysis

**Full citation:** Egami, Naoki, and Erin Hartman. 2023. "Elements of External Validity: Framework, Design, and Analysis." American Political Science Review 117 (3): 1070–88.

**Contributor:** Haerin Lee

---

## 1. What the paper establishes

External validity, the generalizability of causal findings, is widely discussed in the social sciences but rarely incorporated into research design or analysis (p. 1070). The paper categorizes it into four dimensions--populations (X), treatments (T), outcomes (Y), and contexts (C)--and ties each dimension to an explicit causal assumption. It then distinguishes two goals of generalization: for effect-generalization, the paper offers three classes of estimators for X and C, whereas for T and Y it depends almost entirely on designing treatments and outcomes close to their targets. For sign-generalization, which holds under weaker assumptions, the paper recommends designing purposive variations into the study and jointly testing the estimates across those variations with a partial conjunction test.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper makes claims about the size of the treatment effect in a population other than its sample without reporting the target population average treatment effect (T-PATE).** The paper should either estimate the T-PATE or explain why it cannot. If the paper reports only the sample average treatment effect (SATE), its confidence interval understates the uncertainty about the effect in the target population even when the two point estimates are close, because estimating the T-PATE must also account for differences between the sample and the target population.

**(b) If a paper generalizes its treatment effect estimate to a target population, check whether it adjusts for pretreatment variables that plausibly affect both selection into the experiment and the size of the treatment effect.** The sample and the target population may differ, but once the observed variables are adjusted for, any remaining differences must be unrelated to the size of the treatment effect.

**(c) If a paper transports its treatment effect estimate to a context with no experimental data, check whether it names the context moderators and explains why other differences between the contexts would not change the treatment effect.** The transported estimate is valid only if the context changes the effect through the named moderators alone. If any difference other than the named moderators also changes the effect, the estimate for the new context is biased. As no randomization design can rule out such differences, the paper must use substantive knowledge of the contexts to argue that the named moderators are sufficient.

**(d) If a paper generalizes the size of its effect to a treatment or outcome it did not measure, check whether it argues that its design closely matches the target.** For example, a survey experiment may measure support for a hypothetical policy but attempt to explain real-world behavior in its conclusion. Unless its design supports T- or Y-validity, there is no general analysis-stage correction that can justify this extrapolation. For a study already fielded, a second-best is to scale the claim down to the direction of the effect and test its sign across the treatments or outcomes it did measure. This second-best option can establish the sign of the target effect but not its size.

**(e) If a paper generalizes the direction of its effect to an unmeasured target through several measured variations, check whether it includes variations expected to produce both smaller and larger effects than the target.** If every variation lies on one side of the target, the target effect may fall outside the estimated range. For example, if a paper uses only low-risk outcomes to support a claim about the effect on high-risk behavior, the sign claim rests on extrapolation even when every estimate is significant.

**(f) If a paper supports a general claim by counting how many of its estimates are significant, check whether it adjusts the count with partial conjunction p-values.** For instance, a paper may write that the effect is significant in 9 of 12 studies or outcomes by counting each estimate with $p < 0.05$. This approach overstates how many of them have a real effect, as the probability that some estimates with no true effect fall below 0.05 by chance increases with the number of variations tested. The paper should ideally report the adjusted p-values for every threshold to show how many variations have a genuine effect.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** "[W]e recommend estimating the T-PATE formally and comparing it against the SATE estimate" (p. 1083). Estimating the T-PATE is important because "bias in the SATE estimates can be large in many applications" (p. 1083), and because "estimates of the T-PATE have larger standard errors compared with that of the SATE" (p. 1083). When the T-PATE cannot be estimated, the paper asks researchers to explain why "effect- and sign-generalization are not possible" (p. 1076).

**For (b).** The paper states that bias from the difference between the sample and the target population "can be addressed when selection into the experiment and treatment effect heterogeneity are unrelated to each other after controlling for pretreatment covariates X" (p. 1073). The formal version appears at Assumption 1 (p. 1073). Based on Assumption 1, the paper argues that variables affecting both sampling and treatment effect heterogeneity should be adjusted for, and further points out that "the required assumption is violated when unobserved variables affect both sampling and treatment effect heterogeneity" (p. 1073). In its reanalysis of Broockman and Kalla (2016) to estimate the T-PATE, the paper warns that Assumption 1 is untenable "[i]f unobserved variables, such as political interest, affect both sampling and effect heterogeneity" (p. 1083).

**For (c).** To transport an estimate to a new context, "researchers need to assume that contexts affect outcomes only through measured context moderators" (p. 1075). The paper formalizes this as Assumption 4 (p. 1075). Assumption 4 fails if "there are other channels through which contexts affect outcomes and moderate treatment effects" (p. 1075). The paper notes that no general randomization design can make Assumption 4 true, and compares it to the exclusion restriction in instrumental variables, which "needs justification based on domain knowledge" (p. 1075).

**For (d).** The paper emphasizes "designing treatments and measuring outcomes as similar as possible to their targets" (p. 1079) to secure T- and Y-validity. It further warns that "[i]f such design-based solutions are not available, there is no general approach to conducting effect-generalization for T- and Y-validity without making stringent assumptions" (p. 1080). Since the required assumptions, formalized in Assumptions 2 and 3 (p. 1074), are "much more difficult to justify after experiments are completed" (p. 1077), the paper offers a second-best: "sign-generalization is more appropriate for addressing T- and Y-validity when researchers cannot modify their experiment to satisfy the required assumptions" (p. 1077).

**For (e).** The paper shows that "when causal effects are positive (negative) for all K outcomes, the causal effect on the target outcome is also positive (negative) under the range assumption" (p. 1081). The range assumption is formalized as Assumption 5 (p. 1081). Without the range assumption, significance on every variation does not settle the sign of the target effect. The paper recommends choosing "a range of outcomes on which treatment effects are expected to be smaller and larger than the T-PATE" (p. 1081). In the paper's example of Young (2019), "if the target outcome is a real-world high-risk dissent behavior and the intervention effect on this outcome is much smaller than those studied in the experiment, the range assumption is violated" (p. 1081).

**For (f).** Partial conjunction p-values help researchers "assess the direction of the T-PATE while accounting for multiple comparisons correctly" (p. 1080). Rejecting the partial conjunction null at a threshold r provides "statistical evidence that the treatment has positive causal effects on at least r outcomes" (p. 1082). Figure 6 shows why a raw count misleads: two of its three outcomes have p-values below 0.05 before adjustment, but only one does after (p. 1082). Hence, the paper recommends "reporting partial conjunction p-values ... for every threshold r" (p. 1082).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** research that extends a causal claim beyond the population, treatment, outcome, or context it actually studied. The paper defines external validity only against targets the researcher specifies, noting that "[s]pecifying targets is equivalent to clarifying studies' scope conditions" (p. 1072). Hence, every check is triggered by what the research claims, not by what it measured. Reviewers should read the target off the study's own claims, including implicit ones, but should not hold a study to a target it never claims (p. 1073). A policy recommendation in the conclusion counts as such a claim even when the methods section describes a narrow sample. Checks (a) through (d) apply when the claim concerns the size of the effect (p. 1076). Checks (e) and (f) apply when the claim concerns only the direction of the effect, and they can be applied across any of the four dimensions (p. 1082).

**Does not apply to:** research whose claims stay within the study's own sample, treatment, outcome, and context, as its SATE is the target. In this case, all six checks should stay silent. Theory-testing papers that state hypotheses only as signs do not activate checks (a) through (d) (p. 1076); for those papers, (e) and (f) are still applicable.

Check (b) does not apply when the sample is a random sample of the target population, which the paper treats as a special case where no adjustment is required (p. 1073). It also does not apply when the paper shows that treatment effects are homogeneous (p. 1073).

Check (c) does not apply to papers whose experiments already include the new context. The difference between checks (b) and (c) is whether the variable varies within the experiment: "if the variable has some variation, it is an X-validity problem, and it is a C-validity problem otherwise" (p. 1075).

Checks (d) and (e) do not apply when the study measures the target outcome itself, as Assumption 3 "naturally holds if researchers measure the target outcome in the experiment" (p. 1074).

Check (e) does not apply when a study presents multiple outcomes or sites only as robustness within the range it observed; it triggers only when the claim reaches a target outside that range. Since more than 80% of experimental articles in the APSR from 2015 to 2019 include variation on at least one dimension (p. 1080), triggering on variation alone would make (e) fire on nearly every experiment.

Check (f) does not apply when a study requires every estimate to be significant. The paper shows that checking whether all $K$ estimates are significant "is valid as a sign-generalization test, without additional multiple testing corrections" (p. 1081).

**Partial application:** observational studies. The paper argues that the same four dimensions of external validity apply. However, "effect-generalization requires even stronger assumptions" in observational studies, while sign-generalization remains possible wherever purposive variations exist in the data (p. 1086). Therefore, checks (a) through (d) should flag the concern without demanding the experimental estimators, while (e) and (f) apply under the same conditions as in experimental studies.

## 5. What failure looks like

A manuscript reports a difference in means from an online panel, then closes with a sentence such as "this intervention could reduce prejudice among voters nationwide," with no T-PATE and no explanation of why T-PATE cannot be estimated. Its heterogeneity analysis shows that the effect is concentrated among politically inattentive respondents, and its descriptive table shows that the sample is more attentive than the target population, yet its weighting appendix adjusts only for age, gender, race, and education, without mentioning attentiveness. A discussion paragraph extends the result to another country without naming any context moderator. The measures section lists only hypothetical items, while the conclusion describes real-world behavior without arguing that the items closely match that behavior or span its likely range. A robustness table marks most outcomes as statistically significant, and the text treats the share of significant estimates as evidence of a general effect, without adjusted p-values. There is no paragraph in the study naming which dimension of external validity cannot be addressed and why.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Kao, Jay C. 2026."How the Pro-Beijing Media Influences Voters: Evidence from a Randomized Field Experiment." *American Political Science Review* 120(3): 795--814. Checks (a), (b), and (d) should trigger because the article applies the persuasion rate from two weeks of incentivized browsing to two million incidentally exposed voters to estimate a counterfactual vote share, without a T-PATE or adjustment for the partisan heterogeneity it reports (p. 809). The article acknowledges that this calculation rests on several assumptions (p. 809), but acknowledging them does not address the gaps in X- and T-validity.

**Should NOT trigger:** DeMora, Stephanie L., Jennifer L. Merolla, Brian Newman, and Elizabeth J. Zechmeister. 2024. "Jesus was a Refugee: Religious Values Framing can Increase Support for Refugees Among White Evangelical Republicans." *Political Behavior* 46: 2145--2168. A near-miss with a national sample and several outcomes, but its hypotheses predict only the direction of the effect within the subpopulation it samples (pp. 2151); thus, checks (a) through (d) should not trigger. Check (e) stays silent because its outcomes are the attitudes the hypotheses concern, leaving no unmeasured target, and check (f) does not trigger as the paper names which outcomes moved and which did not, rather than counting significant estimates as evidence of a general effect (p. 2156).
