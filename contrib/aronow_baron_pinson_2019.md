# Dropping Experimental Subjects Who Fail a Manipulation Check

**Contributor:** Sang-un Youn

**Full citation:** Aronow, Peter M., Jonathon Baron, and Lauren Pinson. 2019. “A Note on Dropping Experimental Subjects who Fail a Manipulation Check.” _Political Analysis_ 27(4): 572–589. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2019.5.

## 1. What the paper establishes

Manipulation checks are often considered a way to produce more reliable and robust outcomes when inferring treatment effects under experimental conditions. This paper, however, argues that dropping subjects based on a manipulation check after treatment assignment may increase bias in the experimental outcome. Likewise, an analysis that includes subjects who failed the manipulation check can produce a consistent estimate of a well-defined causal effect.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether a paper conducting an experimental study explicitly notes that it included a manipulation check in its design to exclude certain subjects.** Some papers or dissertations may describe their research procedures vaguely, making it difficult for reviewers to identify whether the experiment included a manipulation check in the first place. The author may note that they conducted a manipulation check or a similar post-treatment measure anywhere in the paper, including the footnotes, appendices, or figures.

**(b) Check whether the paper's treatment does not affect whether a subject passes the manipulation check.** For instance, a treatment that induces different degrees of stress or exposes subjects to the treatment for different lengths of time may affect the manipulation check and should therefore be considered when assessing this check. A manipulation check that depends on the treatment may cause more bias rather than prevent it when subjects who fail the check are dropped. This may be the case when the treatment requires specific skills or knowledge for its effect to be clearly applied to subjects.

**(c) Check whether the paper excluded subjects who failed the manipulation check _after_ treatment assignment.** The order of the manipulation check and treatment assignment is important for assessing the independence between treatment assignment and the manipulation check. Excluding subjects who failed a post-treatment check may affect the estimated outcome because those subjects may still be affected by the treatment even though they failed the manipulation check.

**(d) Check whether the paper explicitly justifies dropping subjects after treatment assignment by showing that the proportions dropped were identical across groups.** This may still create bias in the outcome because independence between the manipulation check and the treatment effect alone does not imply that the average treatment effect among those who passed the manipulation check is identical or consistent across treatment conditions.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** "[R]esearchers may be inclined to exclude those subjects who fail the manipulation check from further analysis. This practice is common across the social sciences" (p. 572). The authors further note that this practice has appeared in "top journals in multiple disciplines over recent years, including the _American Political Science Review_" (p. 572). However, Footnote 1 on p. 572 notes that the authors sometimes had to _suspect_ that a study included a manipulation or attention check and contact its authors by email to verify whether it did, meaning that a reviewer may not be able to tell from the paper whether its design included a manipulation check.

**For (b).** Among the three assumptions the authors make to support their claim, the second concerns _ignorability_: "the treatment is not systematically related to potential outcomes or potential manipulation check results, as would be ensured by random assignment of the treatment" (p. 573). They use Corollaries 1 (p. 573) and 2 (p. 573) to clarify that treatment assignment should be unrelated to potential manipulation-check outcomes. The same logic applies when using covariates, as they also have to be unrelated to the manipulation check, as noted in Assumption 4 and Corollary 4 (p. 576).

This does not mean that the relationship between treatment and potential manipulation-check results should _always_ be independent or that subjects should _never_ be excluded, because discarding subjects who are unable to pass manipulation checks can sometimes be "unproblematic if the inferential target is the average potential outcomes among those who pass the manipulation check" (p. 574), depending on the outcome the researcher wishes to observe.

**For (c).** The same evidence used for (b) can also be used for (c), as the author explicitly argues that subjects may be dropped "if a check precedes treatment (e.g., a pretreatment attention check), then discarding subjects is not a problem, at least for characterizing effects for a well-defined subpopulation of units" (p. 574). This way, the reader can be assured that the research design ensures independence between treatment assignment and the manipulation check, as stated in Corollaries 1 (p. 574) and 2 (p. 574), along with Assumption 4 (p. 576) and Corollary 4 (p. 576), which apply to a vector of covariates.

**For (d).** Identical proportions of manipulation-check failures do not establish that the outcome is robust, "because the types of subjects who fail the manipulation check under one treatment may not be the same as those who fail under a different treatment" (p. 575). This follows from the fact that although the treatment may affect which subjects pass the manipulation check, identical failure rates do not show that the same types of subjects fail across treatment conditions; independence between treatment assignment and the manipulation check does not _ensure_ that the average treatment effect among those who pass the check is the same as the effect for a group that includes both passers and non-passers, as noted in Corollaries 3 (p. 575) and 5 (p. 576).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** An experimental or survey study in which the author does not clearly disclose whether they conducted a manipulation check or another post-treatment test to exclude subjects who did not meet the study's standards. As such, check (a) should be applied to a paper that is vague about whether it used manipulation checks or a similar post-treatment measure. The paper formalizes the problem of manipulation or user-attention checks for a finite set of treatment conditions under SUTVA, randomized/ignorable assignment, and positive passing rates in every condition, as stated in Assumptions 1 (p. 573), 2 (p. 574), and 3 (p. 574).

**Applies to:** An experimental or survey study in which the researcher has reason to check how participants understand the treatment. This may involve a treatment that conveys a specific message through text, video, or a picture. The messages that the researcher intends to convey may involve vague policy issues that require interpretation, such as 'protecting jobs' or 'restricting trade,' and may require a distinct procedure to verify whether subjects properly understood the message. Checks (a), (b), and (c) should apply to any such paper. Check (d) should apply only when the paper explicitly acknowledges that it intentionally excluded subjects who failed a manipulation check or a similar post-treatment test after treatment assignment.

**Does not apply to:** A paper that conducts a manipulation or attention check before treatment assignment, when exclusion defines a pre-treatment subpopulation and does not condition on a treatment-affected variable.

**Does not apply to:** Quantitative research that uses observational data instead of an experiment or survey to measure the treatment effect the researcher wishes to examine.

**Partial application:** This applies to a research design that measures background knowledge or expertise in a specific policy area. If the researcher tries to exclude subjects who have insufficient background knowledge, this check would not apply. If the researcher wants to use it as a control or explanatory variable, this check would not be applicable.

## 5. What failure looks like

A paper conducts an experiment or survey and explicitly notes that participants who answered a treatment-comprehension question incorrectly were removed, while the main table or figure compares outcomes among the remaining participants. The paper may also conclude that randomization between the control and treatment groups is preserved without reporting the intention-to-treat estimate for the full sample. It may also describe the contrast among those who passed the manipulation check as the “effect among attentive participants” but provide no identification argument or bounds for the population that would pass under every treatment condition. A reviewer should ask for the all-assigned estimate and a clear statement of the restricted estimate’s target; if the authors retain an always-passer causal claim, they should provide a defensible identification strategy or report the paper’s bounds and their limitations.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Kim, Park, Rhee, and Joonseok Yang. 2023. "Target, Information, and Trade Preferences: Evidence from a Survey Experiment in East Asia." _American Journal of Political Science_ 67(4): 898–914. Survey participants from three countries (China, Japan, and South Korea) were exposed to three treatments and a separate control group to assess which treatment had the most influence on support for protectionism (p. 904). The article uses quota sampling based on the participants' demographic characteristics, but although the paper states that the "sample excludes any respondents who did not pass the Qualtric’s quality checks" (p. 905), it does not indicate whether these checks came after treatment assignment; this should therefore trigger checks (b) and (c).

**Should NOT trigger:** Jin Woo Kim. 2025. "Evidence Can Change Partisan Minds, but Less So in Hostile Contexts." _British Journal of Political Science_ 55(e62): 1–21. The paper conducts two survey experiments to examine whether exposure to information presented in an adversarial manner provokes more resistance to political persuasion than information without such emotional framing. The paper notes that screening out non-U.S. citizens and administering a manipulation question took place before treatment assignment (p. 8), and further notes in Footnote 3 that those who answered an additional screening question after the first one were "not excluded from the main analyses in both Studies 1 and 2" (p. 8), regardless of whether they passed the test. The paper provides these details in an appendix. Because the author states that all screening took place before treatment assignment and that subjects who failed the test were retained in the main analysis, Kim's survey experiment does not directly apply.
