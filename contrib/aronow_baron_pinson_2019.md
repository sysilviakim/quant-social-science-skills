# Dropping Experimental Subjects Who Fail a Manipulation Check

**Full citation:** Aronow, Peter M., Jonathon Baron, and Lauren Pinson. 2019. “A Note on Dropping Experimental Subjects who Fail a Manipulation Check.” *Political Analysis* 27(4): 572–589. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2019.5.

**Contributor:** Sang-un Youn

## 1. What the paper establishes

Manipulation checks are often considered as a process of producing more reliable and robust outcomes for inferring the effect of the treatment under experimental conditions. This paper, however, argues that processing a manipulation check through dropping the subjects after the treatment has been assigned may lead to an increased bias in producing the experimental outcome. Likewise, if the experimental analysis was conducted based on subjects that includes those who failed the manipulation test, they produce a consistent estimand that infers a well-defined causal effect.

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** Randomized experiments in which researchers use a manipulation-check result measured after assignment to exclude observations or define the analyzed sample. Specify whether each check applies to binary or multiple treatment arms, the outcome type, and the estimand only to the extent the article supports it. The paper’s results concern post-treatment selection and principal strata defined by potential manipulation-check outcomes.

**Does not apply to:** **Does not apply, or should stay silent:** A manipulation/attention check measured before treatment assignment, when exclusion defines a pretreatment subpopulation and does not condition on a treatment-affected variable. Also distinguish a manuscript that reports manipulation-check pass rates but retains all randomized subjects in its primary analysis from one that uses those results to select the analysis sample.

**Important limits:** A nonsignificant test of pass-rate differences, or balance on observed covariates among passers, does not by itself establish that exclusion is harmless. Conversely, do not claim that every exclusion based on every post-treatment variable necessarily creates bias in every setting: state the target estimand, design, and assumptions. The article also notes that instrumental-variable strategies require assumptions, including an exclusion restriction, that are not generally verifiable from the experiment (p. 574). **[Decide whether that point merits its own narrow check; do not include it just to increase the number of checks.]**

## 5. What failure looks like

Describe a concrete manuscript pattern, not just a methodological concern. Possible signatures to verify against the paper’s framework:

- Methods state that participants who failed a manipulation check administered after exposure were removed before estimating treatment effects, but the paper gives no account of how selection changes the estimand.
- The authors point to similar pass rates across treatment arms as proof that exclusion cannot bias the treatment comparison.
- The analysis claims to estimate an effect among “attentive” or “comprehending” participants without defining that population in terms of potential pass status under all treatment conditions or addressing identification.
- A balance table or nonsignificant balance test among passers is presented as sufficient proof that conditioning on passing is harmless.
