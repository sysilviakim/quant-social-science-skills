# Treatment-Control Comparisons in Natural Experiments

**Contributor: Yang Jaepyo**

**Full citation:** Sekhon, Jasjeet S., and Rocío Titiunik. 2012. “When Natural Experiments Are Neither Natural nor Experiments.” *American Political Science Review* 106(1): 35–57. doi:10.1017/S0003055411000542.

## 1. What the paper establishes

Even if a natural intervention is randomly assigned, the treatment and control groups chosen by a researcher are not automatically comparable. The treatment-control comparison that randomization guarantees may not estimate the effect the researcher wishes to study. The paper shows this by examining four different natural experiments in the existing literature.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether the treatment and comparison groups came from the same random assignment process.** If the comparison group was not subject to the same randomization, random assignment does not establish comparability between the groups.

**(b) If a study identifies itself as a natural experiment and relies on as-if random assignment, check whether the treatment and comparison groups have similar pretreatment outcomes.** If the pretreatment outcomes are not similar, we should question the as-if randomness assumption. Additionally, check whether the authors adjust for those differences using methods such as matching. If so, check whether the post-adjustment balance between the treatment and comparison groups improves, and whether placebo tests still show an apparent effect where the true effect should be zero.

**(c) When comparing the same units before and after the treatment, check whether the authors address other factors that could have changed the outcome over time even without the treatment.** The fact that we are comparing the same units does not mean that all the change is due to the treatment. We need to check other factors such as partisan tides or differences between on-year and off-year elections.

**(d) When a study uses only units untreated at *t*+1 to estimate the effect of treatment at *t*, check whether the authors address possible interactions between the earlier and later treatment assignments.** It is possible that the effect of the earlier treatment differs by the later treatment assignment. Being untreated in the later period does not mean that the unit is unaffected by the later intervention.

**(e) In a close-election RD, after making comparable groups, check whether some observations are excluded due to post-treatment information such as retirement or whether the candidate runs again.** If exclusion rates differ between the two groups, the comparability established by the original RD may break down.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** Ansolabehere, Snyder, and Stewart (2000) use redistricting to compare voters moved into a new district with voters already living there in order to estimate the personal vote (p. 37). Sekhon and Titiunik point out that “none of the voters in district B can participate in this randomization, and hence they are not guaranteed to be comparable to district A’s voters” (p. 37). Only voters originally in district A were subject to the randomization, while voters originally in district B did not participate in that process (p. 37). Therefore, “randomization says nothing about the comparability of B’s new voters and B’s old voters” (p. 37).

**For (b).** Sekhon and Titiunik test the as-if randomness assumption by comparing pretreatment incumbent vote shares of units that would later be redistricted and units that would not (p. 40). “Because the outcome examined is the incumbent vote share before redistricting occurs, the true effect is zero by construction” (p. 40). But the observed pretreatment outcomes were different: “the empirical quantiles of the baseline incumbent vote share for would-be treatments are everywhere larger than the empirical quantiles for would-be controls” (p. 40). This means the treatment and comparison groups were not similar in this pretreatment outcome, which amounts to questioning the assumption of as-if randomization.

The authors address this problem by using a matching method to adjust for differences between the treatment and comparison groups (p. 42). The adjustment substantially improves covariate balance (p. 42). “Table 1 shows that the treatment and control groups used in this placebo test looked very different before controlling for the covariates, but after the matching procedure they look indistinguishable in all the covariates described earlier” (p. 42).

**For (c).** Grofman, Griffin, and Berry (1995) compare the same House members before and after they move to the Senate (p. 46). Sekhon and Titiunik note that “this over-time comparison is only meaningful under the assumption that moving House members would not have changed their behavior between *t*-1 and *t* if they had stayed in the House” (p. 46). Even when comparing the same House members before and after the treatment, a counterfactual assumption is required that their behavior would not have changed if they had not moved to the Senate (p. 46). Therefore, a same-unit comparison does not necessarily justify interpreting all observed changes as treatment effects. They give partisan tides and differences between on-year and off-year elections as examples of factors that may change over time and affect legislative behavior (p. 46). “This stability assumption is not guaranteed by randomization” (p. 46).

**For (d).** Bhavnani (2009) estimates the effect of the 1997 reservations for women on the 2002 election using only wards that were open in 2002 (p. 47). “But this independence of treatment assignments does not imply that the effects of the two treatments do not interact with each other” (p. 47). The effect of the 1997 treatment may vary depending on the 2002 assignment (p. 47). Sekhon and Titiunik point out that “the design assumes that wards that are open in 2002 would have had the same outcomes if there had been no reservations assigned in 2002” (p. 47). Thus, units untreated in 2002 do not automatically represent the counterfactual world in which the 2002 intervention did not occur. The authors say “it is impossible to decide whether or not to subset the data without specifically assuming how the effects of the 1997 and 2002 allocations of reservations interact with one another” (p. 49).

**For (e).** Butler (2009) uses a close-election RD to study the effect of tenure and drops districts where an incumbent does not run in election *t* or *t*+1 (p. 51). Sekhon and Titiunik note that “assuming winners are randomly assigned guarantees that all bare-winner districts are comparable to all bare-loser districts” (p. 52). Random assignment therefore guarantees comparability only between the original groups, so we should check whether the remaining subset is still comparable after exclusions. The post-treatment exclusion was asymmetric: “20% of incumbents who barely win reelection retire in the next election, whereas no victorious challengers who barely win retire” (p. 52). Because retirement removes observations at different rates from the two groups, keeping only districts in which the incumbent runs again at *t*+1 may break the comparability established by the original RD. By contrast, if open-seat status at *t* is determined before randomization, restricting the sample to incumbent-held districts at *t* does not affect treatment-control comparability (p. 52).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** natural experiments or quasi-random designs in which researchers justify a causal comparison based on random or as-if random assignment, especially when they need to decide which groups to compare after the intervention. Natural experiments can create several possible treatment and comparison groups, but only some of them are comparable even under random assignment (pp. 36–38). Check (a) is relevant when a study justifies a particular comparison among several possible treatment and comparison groups. Check (b) applies when a study relies on an as-if random assignment assumption. The reviewer should examine available pretreatment outcomes or covariates to assess the credibility of that assumption (p. 40). Check (c) applies when the same units are compared before and after treatment and the observed change is interpreted as a causal effect (p. 46). Check (d) applies when treatment occurs in multiple periods and the researcher restricts the sample to units untreated in a later period to estimate the effect of an earlier treatment (pp. 47, 49). Check (e) applies to close-election RD designs when post-treatment information such as retirement or candidacy determines whether an observation remains in the sample (pp. 51–52).

**Does not directly apply to:** Check (a) when the researcher defines the treatment and control groups in advance and then randomly assigns subjects to those groups, as in a standard randomized controlled experiment (pp. 35–36). The concern in Check (c) does not arise when separate treatment and control groups are compared at the same point in time rather than the same units being compared over time. Check (d) is not triggered when later treatment status is not used to restrict the sample. Check (e) is not relevant when a close-election RD does not exclude observations based on post-treatment information.

**Partial application:** known-randomization natural experiments. Check (b) should stay silent when treatment assignment is actually known to be random rather than only assumed to be as-if random, but other checks can still apply. In the Mumbai reservation case, for example, Check (d) remains relevant despite known random assignment (pp. 46–47).

**Boundary case:** pre-randomization sample restrictions in close-election RD. Check (e) concerns post-treatment sample restrictions, not sample restrictions in general. If open-seat status at *t* is determined before randomization, Check (e) should stay silent because restricting to incumbent-held districts can preserve comparability (p. 52).

## 5. What failure looks like

A manuscript may describe an intervention as natural or random and then treat differences between researcher-chosen groups as causal without showing that both groups arose from the same assignment process. In redistricting studies, for example, differences in incumbent vote shares between newly added and previously represented areas may be interpreted as the personal vote. An as-if-random claim may also rest only on a covariate balance table, with no pretreatment-outcome placebo testing whether the groups already differed before treatment. Sekhon and Titiunik’s redistricting application illustrates this problem: the original balance evidence compares party strength, while their placebo uses pretreatment incumbent vote, whose true treatment effect should be zero. A before-and-after design may report changes for the same units without a contemporaneous counterfactual. In multi-period settings, the main analysis may condition on units untreated later without testing whether the earlier effect varies by later treatment status. Finally, a close-election RD may exclude observations based on post-treatment candidacy or retirement while reporting only the restricted-sample estimate and total N, without side-specific exclusion rates or robustness to alternative inclusion rules. Butler’s sample construction and main results table have this structure.

## 6. Test cases

**SHOULD trigger:** Uppal, Yogesh. 2009. “The Disadvantaged Incumbents: Estimating Incumbency Effects in Indian State Legislatures.” *Public Choice* 138(1–2): 9–27. The paper uses close-election regression discontinuity to estimate incumbency effects, but its main conditional estimates are restricted to candidates who run again in the following election. Bare winners are about 14 percentage points more likely to rerun than bare losers before 1991 and about 10 percentage points more likely afterward (p. 16). Because rerunning is determined after the close election, check (e) should trigger: the comparability of bare winners and bare losers around the cutoff may not survive this selection. Uppal recognizes the potential sample-selection problem and compares rerunning and nonrerunning losers on predetermined characteristics (Table 3, p. 21). However, this diagnostic does not by itself establish that the selected bare winners and bare losers remain comparable.

**Should NOT trigger:** Chattopadhyay, Raghabendra, and Esther Duflo. 2004. “Women as Policy Makers: Evidence from a Randomized Policy Experiment in India.” *Econometrica* 72(5): 1409–1443. A near-miss: reservations for women rotate across elections, and the paper uses future reservation status in a robustness check, so a careless agent may want to trigger check (d). However, the main analysis compares public-goods outcomes in Gram Panchayats (GPs) randomly reserved for women with those in unreserved GPs from the same assignment process. The authors reconstruct and verify the reservation procedure in West Bengal (p. 1414), and estimate the main effect by comparing mean outcomes between currently reserved and unreserved GPs (pp. 1425–1426). Although a robustness check compares GPs reserved in 1998 with those scheduled for reservation in 2003 to examine reelection incentives (pp. 1437–1439), the main effect is not estimated by restricting the sample to units untreated in a later period. Triggering check (d) merely because reservations rotate across elections would be a false positive.
