# Interpretation of Fixed Effects Regression Results

**Full citation:** Mummolo, Jonathan and Peterson, Erik. 2018. “Improving the Interpretation of Fixed Effects Regression Results” *Political Science Research and Methods* 6(4): 829–835. doi:10.1017/psrm.2017.44.

**Contributor:** Hyunsu Kim

---

## 1. What the paper establishes

Linear fixed effects regression reduces time-invariant confounding in the estimation of causal effects in observational data and estimates a variable’s average effect within units over time. The paper argues that literature using fixed effects often fails to acknowledge the variance reduction. In a review of 54 studies in APSR and AJPS (2008–2015), the authors found that roughly 69% described the variation used for estimation, but only one study quantified the size of typical shifts in levels of the treatment within units. Replicating five articles, they showed that fixed effects reduce the standard deviation of the treatment and proposed a checklist for interpreting fixed effects results.

## 2. Checks for a reviewer

**(a) Check whether the counterfactual shift in X describing the effect is justified by the within-unit variation used for estimation.**

**(b) Check whether the paper identifies how large the described shift in X is relative to the within-unit variation.** Knowing this ratio (a multiple of the residualized standard deviation) lets readers judge how often a change of the described size occurs within units in the real world, and how much weight the substantive significance of the reported effect can bear. In Mummolo and Peterson’s (2018) replications, the large ratio indicates that the change is hard to occur within-units. There is no exact limit for an acceptable ratio.

**(c) Check the paper considers which outcome scale is most relevant, both in the outcome’s units and in standard deviations of the original and transformed outcome.** The substance of the particular study should guide scale is most relevant.

## 3. Evidence

**For (a).** The authors argue that ”such extreme counterfactuals, which extrapolate to regions of sparse (or nonexistent) data rest on strong modeling assumptions, and can produce severely biased estimates of a variable’s effect if those assumptions fail. ” (p. 830) Figuring out the substantive scale ”can improve descriptions of the substantive significance of results.(p. 830). ”

**For (b).** In the replication results from recent studies, the authors compare the counterfactual change in the treatment discussed in each articles with their preferred counterfactual, the revised standard deviation of 𝑋 (Table 1, p. 832). They also indicates that studies using fixed effects should clarify the variation being studied, for example with phrases such as ”as 𝑋 changes within countries over time, 𝑌 changes” (p. 834).

**For (c).** The fourth item of the authors’ checklist recommends characterizing the effect both in the outcome’s units and in standard deviations of the original and transformed outcome. It adds that the substance of the particular study should guide which scale is most relevant (p. 834).

## 4. Scope

**Applies to:** studies that estimate an effect with linear fixed effects and describe the substantive size of that effect for a non-dichotomous treatment.

**Does not apply to:** judging whether the statistical significance or the general conclusions of a reviewed study hold. The authors argue that their statement has no bearing on statistical significance (n. 2).
